Powers follow from $A^{2} = \lambda$ by induction, and splitting the series into even and odd terms gives $\sum_k \lambda^{k}/(2k)! + A \sum_k \lambda^{k}/(2k+1)!$, which are the stated functions of $\theta$ (and $1 + A$ when $\lambda = 0$, the series terminating). The series converges absolutely in the finite-dimensional algebra. Reversion is a continuous anti-automorphism with $\tilde{A} = -A$ (\claimref{std-algebra.involution-properties}), so $(e^{A})^{\sim} = e^{-A}$, and $e^{A}e^{-A} = 1$ since $A$ commutes with $-A$.

Simple bivectors square to scalars. For vectors $a, b$, $a \wedge b = \tfrac12(ab - ba) = ab - B(a, b) = B(a, b) - ba$ by \claimref{std-algebra.product-decomposition} and \claimref{std-algebra.vector-squares}. Hence
\begin{equation}
(a \wedge b)^{2} = (ab - B)(B - ba) = B(ab + ba) - a\, b^{2} a - B^{2} = B(a, b)^{2} - Q(a) Q(b),
\end{equation}
using $ab + ba = 2B(a, b)$ and $a b^{2} a = Q(b) a^{2} = Q(a)Q(b)$; no orthogonal basis is needed. When $n \le 3$ every bivector is simple. By \claimref{std-algebra.exterior-isomorphism} the outer product is that of $\Lambda V$, which does not depend on $Q$, so it suffices that $\Lambda^{2} W$ is decomposable for $\dim W \le 3$. For $\dim W \le 2$ this is clear. For $\dim W = 3$ with basis $e_1, e_2, e_3$, write $\omega = x\, e_2 \wedge e_3 + y\, e_3 \wedge e_1 + z\, e_1 \wedge e_2$. If $z \ne 0$, then $\omega = (z e_1 - x e_3) \wedge (e_2 - \tfrac{y}{z} e_3)$, and the other cases follow by permuting the basis.

Rotors. Take $a$ with $Q(a) = \epsilon = \pm 1$ and $b$ orthogonal to $a$, and absorb the angle into $b$, so that $A = ab = a \wedge b$ (no normalisation of $b$) and $\lambda = A^{2} = -\epsilon Q(b)$ by \claimref{std-algebra.orthogonal-anticommute}. The exponential is $c + s\, ab$ with $(c, s) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta/\theta)$, $(\cosh\theta, \sinh\theta/\theta)$ or $(1, 1)$. Then $c + s\, ab = a\,(\epsilon c\, a + s\, b)$ (since $a^{2} = \epsilon$ and $\epsilon^{2} = 1$): a product of two vectors. The second is invertible, since $Q(\epsilon c\, a + s\, b) = c^{2}\epsilon + s^{2} Q(b) = \epsilon(c^{2} - \lambda s^{2})$, which equals $\epsilon$ in all three cases ($\cos^{2} + \sin^{2}$, $\cosh^{2} - \sinh^{2}$, or $c = 1$, $\lambda = 0$). By \claimref{std-algebra.inverse-of-vector} both factors are invertible, so $e^{A}$ is an even versor with reverse-norm $1$, a rotor.
