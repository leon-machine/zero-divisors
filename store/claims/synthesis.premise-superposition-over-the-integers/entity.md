## formal
\emph{Premise (F3), superposition over the integers.} Grant Premise (F2), with its group $G$ and central bit $c$. Signed configurations (the elements of $G$) can be added, with integer coefficients, and the sign bit acts as $-1$: the algebra of the foundation is
\[
A = \Z[G]/(c + 1),
\]
the integral group ring of $G$ modulo the two-sided ideal generated by $c + 1$.

\emph{Status and cost.} A chosen primitive of the foundation, mathematical and not physical. The coefficient ring is the integers, the ring the unit generates under addition; the rationals and the reals are not assumed here (the first is reached by dividing by the squares of directions, the second needs the premise of completion). Identifying $c$ with $-1$ is part of the choice: in the group ring $c$ is a central element with $c^{2} = 1$, and the other possibility, $c$ acting as $+1$ (the only other integer of square $1$), gives the commutative group ring of the configurations, in which order is forgotten. What it does not say: that the coefficients are a field, or anything about limits. No observation bears on it directly; that quantum superposition is this addition is a reading, argued and not proved.

## technical
The third primitive lets configurations be superposed: the group of directions acts linearly, with integer coefficients, and its sign bit acts as minus one. Formally the algebra is the integral group ring of the group of directions divided by the ideal generated by $c + 1$. The integers are chosen because they are the ring the unit generates; nothing finer is assumed. The choice of $-1$ for the bit is what keeps the order of directions visible, since sending the bit to $+1$ instead collapses the algebra to the commutative group ring of the configurations. From this premise the rule that a vector squares to its quadratic form, and the identification with the Clifford algebra over the integers, are theorems.

## lay
The third starting assumption, like the first two a choice about mathematics and not a claim about nature, is that the on-off patterns can be added together and counted with whole numbers, with the special swap bit counting as minus one. Nothing beyond whole numbers is assumed: fractions come later from division, and the continuum of real numbers needs a separate assumption. With this step the whole-number form of the algebra of directions used throughout the series appears.

## one-liner
Premise (F3): configurations superpose with integer coefficients and the sign bit acting as $-1$, so the algebra of the foundation is $\Z[G]/(c + 1)$; the integers, not a field, are the assumed coefficients.
