## formal
For every real quadratic space $(V, Q)$ a Clifford algebra exists: $\Cl(V, Q) = T(V) / I_Q$, the tensor algebra modulo the two-sided ideal generated by $\{v \otimes v - Q(v)\,1 : v \in V\}$, with $\iota$ the inclusion of $V = T^{1}(V)$ followed by the quotient map. Any two Clifford algebras of $(V, Q)$ are isomorphic by a unique isomorphism compatible with the maps from $V$. Concretely, for any real symmetric Gram matrix $G$ on $\R^{n}$ (degenerate allowed), the operators $c(v) = v \wedge {} + \, \iota_{Gv}$ on the exterior algebra $\Lambda \R^{n}$ satisfy $c(v)^{2} = Q(v)$, and so give a representation $\Cl(V, Q) \to \operatorname{End} \Lambda V$. (That this representation is faithful is shown afterwards, in the blade basis result, by the triangularity of $x \mapsto x \cdot 1$.)

## technical
The Clifford algebra is constructed as the tensor algebra of $V$ divided by the ideal generated by $v \otimes v - Q(v)$; the universal property of the tensor algebra passes to the quotient. Uniqueness up to a unique isomorphism is the usual consequence of a universal property. A concrete representation, valid also for degenerate forms, acts on the exterior algebra: a vector acts by wedging with it plus contracting with $B(v, \cdot)$, and this operator squares to $Q(v)$; the blade basis result shows the representation is faithful.

## lay
The rule "an arrow squared is a number" can always be realised by an actual algebra, and in essentially one way only. One explicit construction lets arrows act on oriented areas and volumes by adding a direction or removing one.

## one-liner
$\Cl(V, Q) = T(V)/\langle v \otimes v - Q(v) \rangle$ exists and is unique up to unique isomorphism; the wedge-plus-contraction operators on $\Lambda V$ give a concrete representation.
