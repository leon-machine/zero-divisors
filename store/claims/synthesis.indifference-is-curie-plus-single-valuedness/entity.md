## formal
Formalise the indifference premise (Premise \claimref{dark-matter.p2-indifference}, in the reading of Fact \claimref{families.imported-indifference-instrument}) as follows. A finite group $G$ acts on a set $D$ of framework data and on a finite set $X$ of components; $G_d$ is the stabiliser of the actual datum $d$. A \emph{construction} is a map $\Phi$ from $D$ to nonempty sets of weight assignments $f : X \to \R$ that is covariant, $\Phi(g d) = g \cdot \Phi(d)$ (it uses nothing but the datum); it is \emph{single-valued} at $d$ if $\Phi(d)$ has one element.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{(Curie.)} $\Phi(d)$ is $G_d$-invariant. If $\Phi$ is single-valued at $d$, its assignment is constant on $G_d$-orbits and it singles out no element, partition or ordering of $X$ that $G_d$ does not fix: the clauses ``equates along symmetry orbits'' and ``forbids selections nothing causes''.
\item \emph{(No licence across orbits.)} The equalities $f(x) = f(y)$ holding for every $G_d$-invariant $f$ are exactly those with $y \in G_d x$, so the clause ``never equates inequivalent components'' is a theorem about what invariance licenses. On a finite orbit the only invariant probability is the uniform one (Haar measure); maximum entropy over all of $X$ is invariant but equates across orbits, so it is strictly stronger than symmetry.
\item \emph{(Residue.)} Without single-valuedness the conclusions of (1) fail: under the swap of a two-element orbit the outcome set of the two selections is invariant and contains no invariant member. Indifference is covariance plus single-valuedness, and single-valuedness is all that it adds to symmetry.
\item \emph{(P2.)} For the towers, $G_d$ is generated by the reversal $(p,q) \mapsto (q,p)$, acting on residues as $r \mapsto -r$ with orbits $\{0\}, \{4\}, \{1,7\}, \{2,6\}, \{3,5\}$. Each equality P2 asserts along $\{2,6\}$ (common ceiling coupling, common asymmetry per mode) is an instance of (1) for one datum, so under this formalisation it holds exactly when the descent is covariant and single-valued in that datum. Applied to the deposited energy per unit charge, the same reasoning gives a common temperature, which the record excludes (Proposition \claimref{dark-tower-matter.mirrors-temperature-p2}); so the cold mirror requires the descent to be many-valued, or not reversal covariant, in that datum. P2's indexical clause (which of $2$, $6$ is visible) does not supply this: read as a fact about the observer's location it leaves the descent single-valued, and read as a fact about the descent's outcome it is already a case of many-valuedness that P2 must then admit datum by datum.
\end{enumerate}

## technical
The series' principle of indifference forbids distinctions that nothing in the framework determines, and is used to equate weights along symmetry orbits. Model a construction as a covariant map from the framework's data to sets of possible outcomes; reading an uncaused distinction as a failure of covariance is a modelling choice, and everything below is relative to it, for a finite symmetry group acting on a finite set of components carrying weights. If the outcome is unique, Curie's principle applies: a unique outcome of symmetric data is symmetric, so weights are equal along orbits and nothing is selected that the symmetry does not fix. The companion clause, that indifference never equates inequivalent components, then needs no premise: the indicator function of an orbit is invariant and separates it from every other orbit, so invariance alone licenses exactly the equalities within orbits. In weight form this is Haar measure on each finite orbit; maximum entropy over the whole set would equate across orbits and is stronger than symmetry.

For weights on finite orbits, what indifference adds to symmetry is therefore single-valuedness of the outcome. This does not cover the use of indifference that selects a structure rather than weights, the principal embedding: there the only single-valued covariant output is the zero map, and selecting the principal embedding takes a measure principle in addition (filed alongside, synthesis.principality-is-genericity). When the outcome is a set (as with spontaneous symmetry breaking), the set is symmetric but no member need be. For the towers, each equality P2 draws between the two reversal partners needs single-valuedness in that datum; the excluded common mirror temperature is a datum where it must fail. P2's indexical clause, which partner is visible, does not by itself make room for that: as a fact about where the observer stands it leaves the descent single-valued, and as a fact about the outcome it is already the many-valuedness that P2 would then have to admit datum by datum.

## lay
The series often argues that two things must be equal because nothing in the theory could tell them apart. This result makes that argument precise and finds its exact cost. If a process has only one possible outcome and treats two things symmetrically, the outcome must treat them alike: that part is just symmetry. But if the process can end in several ways, each individual ending can favour one side, as a ball balanced on a hilltop must roll one way or the other. The extra assumption hidden in the principle is that the outcome was unique. Applied to the two mirror worlds of the series, each equality drawn between them needs its own uniqueness assumption, and the evidence that the mirror world is colder than ours shows that, for its temperature at least, the outcome was lopsided.

## one-liner
For weights on finite orbits and with uncaused distinctions read as failures of covariance, indifference's orbit-equating and no-selection clauses are Curie's principle for a single-valued outcome, its never-equate clause is a theorem, and single-valuedness is its residue beyond symmetry; the cold mirror requires that residue to fail for the deposited energy on the reversal orbit.
