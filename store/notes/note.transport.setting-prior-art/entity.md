## formal
Two bodies of classical material are used, and neither is claimed.

The structure theory of the real Clifford algebras (the isomorphisms $\Cl(p,q) \cong \Mat_k(\mathbb{F})$ or $\Mat_k(\mathbb{F}) \oplus \Mat_k(\mathbb{F})$ with $\mathbb{F} \in \{\R, \C, \HH\}$ determined by $p - q$ mod $8$) goes back to Cartan and to the modern treatments of Atiyah, Bott and Shapiro \cite{ABS1964}, Porteous \cite{Porteous1995} and Lounesto \cite{Lounesto2001}; the quadratic-form background is in Lam \cite{Lam2005} and Chevalley \cite{Chevalley1997}. The computation of the centre and of the sign of $\omega^2$ (Lemmas \claimref{transport.centre} and \claimref{transport.sign}) is folklore, restated here and proved in the primer's toolkit \cite[Section 9]{ButlerT0} because the type-C theorem rests on it. Von Neumann regularity of semisimple rings, on which the exhaustiveness theorem rests, is von Neumann's \cite{vonNeumann1936}; the observation that regularity localises zero divisors at idempotents (Lemma \claimref{transport.regularity-localises-zero-divisors-idempotents}) is elementary and surely known, though we have not found it stated in the Clifford literature in the form used here. The idempotent structure of Clifford algebras is surveyed by Lounesto \cite{Lounesto2001} and, computationally, by Ab{\l}amowicz \cite{Ablamowicz1998}.

The point-level machinery of the type-N section is the \emph{conformal model} of geometric algebra and is thoroughly established. The representation of the points of a quadratic space as null rays in a space two dimensions larger, via the embedding $X(x) = \nO + x + \tfrac12 Q(x)\nInf$, together with the realisation of conformal transformations, inversions included, as versors of $\Cl(p{+}1,q{+}1)$, originates in the program of Hestenes and coworkers \cite{Hestenes2001, HLR2001}, has antecedents in the classical projective-quadric (M\"obius and Lie sphere) constructions of the nineteenth century, was given a comprehensive computational treatment by Dorst, Fontijne and Mann \cite{DFM2007}, is standard in the physics literature through Doran and Lasenby \cite{DoranLasenby2003}, and underlies the algebraic theory of conformal groups via Clifford algebras developed by Angl\`es \cite{Angles2008}. Nothing in Sections \ref{sec:prelim} and \ref{sec:model} is claimed as new. The reader familiar with conformal geometric algebra may skim them for notation; the reader \emph{new} to geometric algebra should begin with the companion primer \cite{ButlerT0}, which develops all of this slowly and with worked examples.
