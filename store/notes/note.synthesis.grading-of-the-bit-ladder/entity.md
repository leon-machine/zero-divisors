## formal
The ladder has four chosen primitives, each a postulate of the foundation, and every other step except the last (argued) is a theorem. The grades are those of the source: \emph{chosen} (assumed), \emph{proved} (a theorem in the store or filed with this note), \emph{argued} (an inference from evidence, not a proof).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[0.] \emph{The bit (chosen: premise (F1)).} One distinction, states $0$ and $1$, combined by comparison, $1 + 1 = 0$: the group $\Z_{2}$, and with the product $1 \cdot 1 = 1$ the field $\mathbb{F}_{2}$. It has no sign: $1 = -1$.
\item[1.] \emph{Independence (chosen, part of (F1); counting proved).} $n$ independent distinctions give the configurations $\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}$ under symmetric difference, $2^{n}$ of them, $\binom{n}{k}$ of grade $k$: the index sets of the blades, with no order and no sign.
\item[2.] \emph{Minimal non-commutativity (chosen: premise (F2)).} A group $G$ generated by the directions and one bit $c \ne 1$, a central extension of $\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}$ by that bit, with $e_{i}e_{j} = c\,e_{j}e_{i}$ for $i \ne j$. Proved from it: $c^{2} = 1$ (for $n \ge 2$), every square and commutator lies in $\{1, c\}$, the squares form a quadratic form $q$ over $\mathbb{F}_{2}$ whose polar form is the commutator, the centre, and for even $n$ an extraspecial group; $q$ fixes the type of the algebra (Theorem \claimref{synthesis.blade-square-signs-f2-quadratic-form}; the group statements in the integer-algebra result filed alongside). The sign $-1$, the signature and a plane of square $-1$ appear here, before any addition.
\item[3.] \emph{Superposition over the integers (chosen: premise (F3)).} The algebra is $\Z[G]/(c + 1)$. Proved: it is free of rank $2^{n}$ with basis the blades, $v^{2} = Q(v)$ for integer vectors (the contraction rule as a theorem), and it has the universal property of the Clifford algebra of the $\pm 1$ form over every commutative ring: $\Cl_{\Z}(p,q)$. Over $\Z$ the orthogonal presentation $\Z[G]/(c + 1)$ and the null (Witt) presentation generate different orders, and only the latter contains matrix units (the note on the two presentations over the integers, on \claimref{std-algebra.cl11-matrix}); the Witt pairs need $\tfrac12$, so the qubit reading, $\Cl(k,k) \cong \Mat_{2^{k}}$ (Theorem \claimref{std-algebra.fock-representation}, stated over $\R$), sits at step 4 for the orthogonal presentation.
\item[4.] \emph{Division (proved, no new primitive).} Inverting the vectors that are not null, $v^{-1} = v/Q(v)$ (Proposition \claimref{std-algebra.inverse-of-vector}), forces the coefficients from $\Z$ to $\Q$ and to nothing smaller; null vectors and zero divisors survive except in $\Cl_{\Q}(0,0)$, $\Cl_{\Q}(0,1)$, $\Cl_{\Q}(0,2)$ (the rational counterpart of Theorem \claimref{transport.exceptional-division-algebras}).
\item[5.] \emph{Completion (chosen: premise (F4)).} The coefficients are extended to a complete ordered field. Proved: such a field is Euclidean, every real quadratic space has an anticommuting basis of squares $1, -1, 0$ with invariant counts (Sylvester's law, derived in geometric algebra, \claimref{synthesis.completion-recovers-the-real-clifford-algebras}(iii), which replaced the imported Fact), and every real Clifford algebra of the store is a rational one of a $\pm 1$ form extended by scalars, or a subalgebra of one. The signs need only a Euclidean field; over $\Q$ square classes remain. Completeness proper is used by limits (the exponential of a bivector and $\pi$; the nilpotent exponential of Theorem \claimref{synthesis.nilpotent-steps-derivative-exponential-and-boost} is a finite sum and needs none) and by roots of odd-degree polynomials (for which a real closed field suffices).
\item[6.] \emph{The number systems inside (proved, after (F4)).} $\R$, $\Cl(0,1) \cong \C$, $\Cl(0,2) \cong \HH$ are the only finite-dimensional associative real division algebras (Frobenius, derived in geometric algebra); split-complex and dual numbers and the matrix algebras sit inside, with the classification by $p - q \bmod 8$ (Theorem \claimref{std-algebra.periodicity}); every finite-dimensional associative real algebra embeds in a corner of some $\Cl(k,k)$.
\item[7.] \emph{Bosons (proved in the store, conditional where stated).} Collective pair modes of the infinite tower obey canonical commutation relations in the limit (Theorem \claimref{synthesis.central-limit-of-tower-fluctuation-states-with-the-kernel-unit}); they need step 5 and a state on the tower, and are not a further primitive.
\item[8.] \emph{The algebra of physics (argued).} That nature uses exactly this structure is the argued reading of the companion note, not a theorem.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Where the stored algebra sits.} The store's standard algebra is stated over $\R$ and so sits at step 5; much of its content is already algebraic and holds over $\Q$ or $\Z$ (the blade products, inverses of vectors, the matrix units of $\Cl(1,1)$ over $\Z$), but its statements do not yet say so, and no stored entity assumes the four premises.

## technical
The ladder grades every step. Four primitives are chosen: the bit (a distinction with $1 + 1 = 0$, repeated independently), minimal non-commutativity (one central sign bit between distinct directions), superposition over the integers, and completion of the coefficients. Everything else is proved: the sign $-1$, the squares $\pm 1$ as a quadratic form over the bit and the type of the algebra follow from the second primitive; the integer Clifford algebra with its contraction rule and universal property from the third; the rationals from dividing by vectors; the signs of every real quadratic form, the recovery of the store's real algebras, Frobenius' list of division algebras and the containment of every finite-dimensional associative algebra from the fourth. The bosons are collective limits on the tower, not a fifth primitive. That geometric algebra is the algebra of physics is argued, not proved. The store's existing algebra is stated over the reals and sits after the completion, though much of it holds over the integers or rationals.

## lay
This note lays out the chain of ideas from a single yes-or-no distinction to the numbers physics uses, and marks each link as an assumption, a proof or an argument. There are four assumptions: a distinction, its repetition with one sign recording order, adding combinations, and filling in the gaps of the number line. Everything else follows by proof, from minus one and whole numbers through fractions and, once the gaps are filled, to the complex numbers and the quaternions. Whether nature really is built this way is an argument from evidence, kept separate.

## one-liner
The bit ladder graded: four chosen primitives, the bit (F1), minimal non-commutativity (F2), superposition over $\Z$ (F3) and completion (F4); proved between them the sign, $\Cl_{\Z}(p,q)$, $\Q$ by division, Sylvester's law and the store's real algebras, Frobenius and universality; argued, that this is the algebra of physics.
