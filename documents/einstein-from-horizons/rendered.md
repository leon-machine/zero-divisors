# Einstein's equations from horizon thermodynamics

**Abstract.** This trail follows the route from the thermodynamics of horizons to Einstein's equations. Black-hole temperature and entropy alone cannot single out Einstein's theory, because a whole family of theories shares the same black holes.

Jacobson's argument closes the gap by applying the heat law $\delta Q = T\,\delta S$ to every small horizon that matter crosses. Under the series' local-horizon premise (C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$), the store proves Einstein's equations along that route, with $\Lambda$ an integration constant and $G_{\mathrm{cl}} = 1/(4\eta)$.

The remaining steps take the premise apart: its temperature clauses are one joint thermal condition, and its third clause adds nothing beyond them.

The walk first says why black holes alone are not enough, then sets out the two imported tools, then proves the equations, and ends by examining what the premise it rests on actually contains.

## What we assume

**Postulate 1 ((C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$): on every local Rindler horizon the entropy of a cut is the integral of a curvature-independent area density (Loc) and, before matter crosses, the modes on the light rays are jointly thermal for the local boost (R$_{\mathrm{loc}}$)).** The series computes a temperature and an entropy for the edges of black holes and of the universe's horizon, using quantum modes it places on those edges. Jacobson showed in 1995 that Einstein's equations of gravity follow if every small piece of horizon around every point, including the horizon seen by an accelerating observer, has a temperature set by the acceleration and an entropy proportional to its area, by the same amount everywhere. This assumption says exactly that, with the quantum modes running continuously along the light rays of each small horizon and being the same field as the matter that falls through it. It has two parts: the entropy of a slice of the horizon is its area times a fixed number that does not depend on how strong the gravitational field is there, and before matter falls through, the modes are thermal at the accelerating observer's temperature. Where there is no gravitational field the second part now follows from a simpler assumption with no temperature in it; the first part is still assumed. With it, the store proves Einstein's equations, with the strength of gravity set by the entropy per area and the cosmological constant left free. The assumption would fail if the entropy per area turned out to depend on the strength of the gravitational field or on matter, or if gravity were found to deviate from Einstein's equations.

## Einstein's equations in the completion

Each step either supplies an ingredient of the thermodynamic route to Einstein's equations or tests the premise that route needs.

We start from what the series already has: black holes with a temperature, a count of modes and an entropy.

Every Lagrangian \[ \frac{R}{16\pi G} + f(\mathrm{Ric}) \] with $f$ at least quadratic in the Ricci tensor shares the Schwarzschild field, its temperature and, assuming Wald entropy, its entropy $A/4G$. So these inputs cannot pick out Einstein's equations; what would separate the theories is entropy on local horizons crossed by matter.

**Theorem 2 (The series' black-hole thermodynamics holds unchanged in every Ricci-polynomial extension of Einstein's theory, so on its own it cannot yield Einstein's equations).** The series has three facts about black holes: their temperature, how many quantum modes they hold, and Hawking's argument that turns the temperature into an entropy. A whole family of theories of gravity, differing from Einstein's in how matter sources the gravitational field, has exactly the same black holes, with the same temperature and, if horizon entropy is given by the standard (Wald) rule, the same entropy. So those three facts, on their own, cannot single out Einstein's equations. The known derivation of Einstein's equations from thermodynamics works because it applies the heat law to tiny horizons that matter is flowing through. The series does not yet say what entropy such a horizon has, and that is the step it would need to supply.

The first tool is geometry.

A bundle of light rays focuses as it travels: its expansion $\theta$ obeys \[ (k \cdot D)\theta = -\theta^{2}/2 - \sigma^{2} + \omega^{2} - \Ric^{\circ}(k,k) \] with $\sigma$ the shear and $\omega$ the twist. Matter and field strength along the rays pull them together, so the area of the bundle shrinks.

**Fact (imported) 3 (Imported: the focusing and shear equations for a null geodesic congruence (Raychaudhuri 1955, Sachs 1961)).** Light rays travelling side by side can converge or spread out. Raychaudhuri and Sachs found the equations that say how a bundle of rays changes as it travels: matter and the gravitational field along the rays focus them together, and a separate part of the field strength squeezes the bundle out of shape. These equations are standard textbook geometry; the series uses them in the step where the energy flowing through a small horizon is converted into the strength of the gravitational field.

*Provenance:* A. Raychaudhuri, Relativistic cosmology. I, Phys. Rev. 98 (1955), 1123--1126.; R. K. Sachs, Gravitational waves in general relativity. VI. The outgoing radiation condition, Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A 264 (1961), 309--338.; R. M. Wald, General Relativity, University of Chicago Press (1984)..

The second tool is thermodynamics.

If every local Rindler horizon has the Unruh temperature, an entropy $\eta$ per unit area, and obeys $\delta Q = T\,\delta S$ for the matter's boost-energy flux, then the gravitational field obeys \[ \mathcal{G}(a) + \Lambda a = -(2\pi/\eta)\,T(a) \] with $\Lambda$ undetermined. This is Jacobson's 1995 result, written in the store's sign.

**Fact (imported) 4 (Imported: Einstein's equation as an equation of state (Jacobson 1995)).** In 1995 Ted Jacobson showed that Einstein's equations can be read as a law of thermodynamics. If every small patch of horizon, as seen by an accelerating observer, has a temperature set by the acceleration and an entropy proportional to its area, and if heat crossing it changes its entropy by heat divided by temperature, then the gravitational field must obey exactly Einstein's equations, read here as the equations of a field on flat spacetime. The strength of gravity is fixed by the entropy per area, and the cosmological constant is left free. This is a known result taken from the literature.

*Provenance:* T. Jacobson, Thermodynamics of spacetime: the Einstein equation of state, Phys. Rev. Lett. 75 (1995), 1260--1263..

The series' local-horizon premise (C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$) supplies the temperature and the entropy, and this step carries the argument through inside the store.

Every further hypothesis is stated: Clausius with $\delta S = \eta\,\delta A$, a thin patch with vanishing expansion and shear, a conserved $T_{ab}$, a $C^{3}$ derived metric, four dimensions. Then \[ \mathcal{R}(a) + 8\pi G_{\mathrm{cl}}\,T(a) = (\Lambda + 4\pi G_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\tau)\,a \] with $\Lambda$ an integration constant and $G_{\mathrm{cl}} = 1/(4\eta)$.

**Theorem 5 (Under (C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$) and the Clausius relation, Jacobson's argument gives Einstein's equations with $\Lambda$ an integration constant and $G_{\mathrm{cl}} = 1/(4\eta)$; every further hypothesis named).** In 1995 Ted Jacobson showed that Einstein's equations of gravity follow if every small patch of horizon, as seen by an accelerating observer, has a temperature set by the acceleration and an entropy proportional to its area, and if heat crossing the patch raises its entropy by heat divided by temperature. The series assumes, as a premise, that its own horizon quantum modes give that temperature and that entropy on all such small horizons. This result writes the argument out step by step under that premise and checks each algebraic step exactly. It also lists everything else the argument needs: that the heat is the energy carried by matter and nothing else changes the entropy, that the horizon patch is momentarily neither expanding nor shearing, that matter is described by an ordinary conserved energy tensor, that the gravitational field is smooth enough, and that space-time has four dimensions. Given all that, Einstein's equations follow, with the strength of gravity fixed by the entropy per area and the cosmological constant left free. What singles out Einstein's equations, rather than more complicated versions of them, is that the entropy per area is the same everywhere and does not depend on the strength of the gravitational field there. The derivation is conditional: it shows what the premise would deliver, not that the premise holds.

The derivation is conditional, so the rest of the walk asks what the premise contains.

Its temperature clauses are one condition: joint KMS for the local boost at rapidity period $2\pi$, a new premise stronger than the local form of (R). Derived is only the geometric half, that this period is where the local boost's Euclidean section is regular, giving \[ T = a/2\pi \] up to a relative correction of order $\rho^{2}$.

**Theorem 6 (On local Rindler horizons and small diamonds, clauses (ii) and (iii) of (C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$), with (iii) read on the patch's whole mode algebra and the boost acting on the modes by phases, are one condition, joint KMS for the local boost at rapidity period $2\pi$, the period at which the local boost's Euclidean section is regular at leading order).** The assumption that every small horizon is hot at the Unruh temperature and that its quantum state's internal clock runs along the horizon's own boost was stated as two separate requirements. The geometry part can be made local: near any point, however strong the gravitational field is, the smoothness condition that sets the temperature at a black hole gives the Unruh temperature, up to small corrections. For a quantum state on a finite set of modes, being thermal for a flow and having that flow as its internal clock are the same property. What is left is one assumption, that the modes of every small horizon are jointly thermal at that temperature. At black holes the series makes the corresponding assumption in two parts, one for each mode separately and one for how the modes combine; the local version needs both, so it is a new assumption, stronger than the black-hole smoothness rule alone. It is not derived here from anything weaker.

Next the entropy clause.

If the entropy density of a small patch depends only on its flat tangent data, not on the field strength or matter at the point, the Lorentz group forces one universal constant $\eta$. Calibrated on the Schwarzschild family under (P), (R) and Hawking's hypothesis, \[ \eta = \tfrac14 \] with no mode count. That independence from curvature excludes the Wald-type entropies of the rival theories met at the start.

**Theorem 7 (If the entropy density of a small horizon patch depends only on its flat tangent data (null ray and edge plane), and not on the curvature, matter or boost normalisation at the point, it is one universal constant; calibrated on the Schwarzschild family under (P), (R) and Hawking's hypothesis, with the hole's entropy taken to be the integral of that density, it is 1/4 with no count).** The local-horizon assumption gives every small horizon an entropy proportional to its area, with the same constant everywhere. This result shows that "the same constant everywhere" is automatic once one assumes that the entropy per area of a small piece of horizon depends only on how the piece sits among the flat directions at its point, and not on how strong the gravitational field is there or what matter is present. That second part is a real assumption: theories of gravity with extra curvature terms give entropies that do depend on the strength of the gravitational field, and this assumption is what rules them out. Given it, a change of viewpoint or of the observer's acceleration turns any small horizon into any other without changing its area, so the entropy per area can only be one number. That number is then fixed by black holes: if a black hole's entropy is the sum of this density over its horizon, and under the series' assumptions about its temperature and Hawking's thermodynamic reasoning, the constant is one quarter, with no need to count modes. The series' mode count gives the same quarter only at the value of its calibration constant that was chosen to match black-hole entropy, as already known.

Putting modes on every horizon raises a bookkeeping question, because the cutoff premises place a black hole's modes at a locus inside its horizon.

If the two placements are independent and the hole's entropy is the total over all its modes, Jacobson's constant becomes $G_{\mathrm{cl}} = 2G$ at the area-law calibration. If they are identified, they inherit the literal Kerr count's conflict with $A_{+}/4$, which counts against the cutoff placement on spinning holes, not against (C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$).

**Theorem 8 ((C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$)(i) is a second placement of the count, not an extension of (C1) and (C2); at a hole's horizon, independent placements double Newton's constant under the total-entropy reading, and the literal Kerr count's conflict with $A_{+}/4$, present in every reading, counts against the cutoff placement, not against (C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$)).** The series places a black hole's hidden quantum modes deep inside the hole, where the curvature reaches the Planck scale, and their number matches the hole's known entropy. This result shows that the two placements never touch: the deep locus lies inside the horizon of every hole heavier than half a Planck mass. So a black hole would have modes in two places. If they are the same set, then for fast-spinning holes the deep count disagrees with the known entropy, as it already does without the new assumption. That disagreement appears whichever way the two sets are related, so it counts against placing the modes deep inside spinning holes, not against the new assumption. The assumption still needs a rule saying how the two sets are related.

The last step looks at the third clause as first written, that the modes' modular flow acts geometrically on nested sub-patches.

A boost acting by phases is almost periodic, so it maps no closed subspace properly into itself and cannot push a smaller patch into a larger one. The clause is consistent only as the boost dynamics on the whole patch, where for a faithful state it restates the temperature clause.

**Theorem 9 (A boost acting by phases moves no C$^{*}$-subalgebra, and in a GNS representation no weakly closed subalgebra, properly into itself, so clause (iii) of (C$_{\mathrm{loc}}$) cannot act geometrically on nested sub-patches of the mode algebra its clause (i) cites).** The new assumption needed for Einstein's equations has three parts: small horizons carry the series' quantum modes, those modes are at the right temperature, and their natural time flow is the geometric flow of the horizon. This result shows that the third part can only be meant in a weak sense. The series' modes evolve by simple rotations of phases, and such an evolution always comes back close to where it started. A genuinely geometric flow would instead push the modes of a smaller piece of horizon ever deeper into a larger piece, never returning. The two cannot both hold. So the third part must be read as a statement about the modes of the whole patch, where it adds nothing beyond the temperature part; the stronger geometric reading would need a continuous family of modes, which the assumption does not name.

**Fact (imported) 11 (Full uniqueness, given the dynamics).** Once the horizon modes' time evolution and the temperature are given, there is exactly one equilibrium state at that temperature, and it is the I-state. This uses a standard result about ideal gases of fermions. It does not explain where the time evolution itself comes from.

**Fact (imported) 12 (Gibbs states are KMS, and conversely, in finite dimensions).** In a finite quantum system, the thermal (Gibbs) state at a given temperature satisfies a standard algebraic test of thermal equilibrium, the KMS condition. This result also proves the converse: in finite dimensions the Gibbs state is the only state that passes the test, even when some energy levels coincide.

**Fact (imported) 13 (Imported: KMS passes to inductive limits).** Thermal equilibrium, in its precise algebraic form, can be checked piece by piece. If a large system is the limit of an increasing chain of smaller subsystems, each preserved by the time evolution, and a state is in equilibrium on every one of them, then it is in equilibrium on the whole system. This is a known result taken from the literature.

**Fact (imported) 14 (KMS restricts to invariant subalgebras).** If a system is in thermal equilibrium and one looks only at a set of observables that the time evolution keeps within itself, those observables are also in equilibrium at the same temperature.

**Fact (imported) 15 (The area-law test).** The framework counts quantum modes that accumulate at a black hole's inner surface over some span of time. The count matches the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, numerical factor included, only if that span is about one natural time unit of the hole, which is also the span causality singles out. Counting over the hole's whole lifetime instead gives far too much, a puzzle shared with other interior-volume pictures and not resolved here.

**Fact (imported) 16 (The epoch is the modular period; two unknowns become one).** The entropy count needed two unknown inputs: how long to count for, and how densely modes are packed. **Fact (imported) 17 (The mode count).** Granting the singularity paper's two placement assumptions (the modes sit on the black hole's inner surface, a fixed number per smallest cell), modes accumulate there as time runs on. To leading order in the ratio of that surface's radius to the hole's size, the number gathered per unit time is proportional to the mass, or equivalently inversely proportional to the hole's Hawking temperature. The overall factor depends on how densely modes are packed, which is left open here.

*Provenance:* Transport Lab claim scrambling.mode-count, version 2 (T08 Thm 3.4).

**Fact (imported) 18 (The Euclidean section, and its period).** Replacing time by an imaginary variable turns the gravitational field outside a black hole into a field on a flat four-dimensional space without time, in which the time direction closes into a circle. Near the horizon the geometry this field describes looks like a flat plane in polar coordinates, but only if the circle has exactly the right length, $8\pi$ times the mass in natural units; any other length leaves a cone point. So the period is fixed by the gravitational field rather than chosen.

*Provenance:* Transport Lab claim singularity.euclidean-section-period, version 5 (T05 Prop 7.18).

**Fact (imported) 19 (KMS uniqueness at a factor).** In a finite quantum system described by a full matrix algebra, a time evolution generated by an operator K singles out equilibrium states, one for each temperature. This result shows that for each nonzero inverse temperature there is exactly one such state, and it is the familiar Gibbs (Boltzmann) state built from K. Unless K is a multiple of the identity (when every temperature gives the same state), the equilibrium states therefore form a single family labelled by temperature.

*Provenance:* Transport Lab claim singularity.kms-uniqueness-factor, version 4 (T05 Thm 7.13).

**Fact (imported) 20 (Modular theory of a matrix factor, proved outright).** Tomita-Takesaki theory attaches to a quantum state a natural time evolution, called the modular flow. For two finite quantum systems in an entangled state, this lemma works out every ingredient of that theory by hand. The modular flow turns out to be the evolution generated by the reduced density matrix of one side, and the state satisfies the equilibrium (KMS) condition with respect to it.

**Fact (imported) 22 (Rate rigidity: the temperature is not algebra intrinsic).** For a single quantum mode with a given time evolution, the symmetries of the algebra that respect the time evolution, or reverse it, can at most flip the sign of an equilibrium state's inverse temperature; they never change its size. Rescaling the clock rescales the temperature while leaving the state itself alone. So the algebra on its own cannot pick out a particular temperature: that needs outside information, namely how the evolution's parameter is converted into physical time.

**Fact (imported) 26 (The standard null-cone form of the Einstein condition, translated into vacuum language: infinity vacua unexcited along their own light rays).** A known way of stating Einstein's condition on empty space is that the curvature felt along every light ray has no part of a certain kind. This result translates that known statement into the series' language of quantum vacua; it does not derive it. In the series, every point of spacetime carries a small family of quantum vacuum states, and so does the point at infinity that a choice of scale singles out. Each such vacuum is attached to one light-ray direction. The vacua of an ordinary point are never disturbed when you move along their own light ray, in any spacetime: that is pure geometry. This result shows that asking the same of the vacua at infinity, that moving along their own light ray never disturbs them, is exactly Einstein's condition on the curvature, with the cosmological constant left free. With matter present, the disturbance along each light ray equals the matter's energy flowing along it exactly when Einstein's equations hold, the cosmological constant appearing as a free constant. These are equivalences: the result translates Einstein's condition into the language of vacua and does not explain why nature would impose it.
