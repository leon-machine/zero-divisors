# Temperature and Hawking radiation

**Abstract.** A black hole glows at a temperature set by its mass, and this trail shows where that number comes from in the framework and what rests on it. The horizon's surface gravity, $\kappa = 1/4M$, is computed from the gravitational field.

Under premises (P) and (R), every boundary mode is thermal at the Hawking temperature. Assuming the interface premise (I), that thermal state reaches a distant observer as Hawking's spectrum with greybody factors.

The second half asks which state the horizon modes are in. The premise (N$_{+}$) says only that they are in a ground state for sliding along the light rays. Its short-distance form now holds for every Hadamard state of the Dirac field, by the theorem (HS). That the series' states are Hadamard is itself an adopted premise, (HD), which does not say which state it is.

The walk starts with the gravitational field, moves to the temperature of a single mode, and follows the radiation out to a distant observer. It then asks how much of the state behind that temperature follows from the field theory, and what is still assumed. Each result appears after the ones it rests on.

## What we assume

**Postulate 1 (Premise (I): the interface identification).** This premise assumes that whatever the horizon state puts into each outgoing channel is carried out to distant observers in the ordinary way. It is an assumption, not a theorem of the framework.

**Postulate 2 ((P): univalence).** This is an assumption, not a theorem. It says that the physical states of the modes living on the boundary give zero average to every quantity that changes fermion number by an odd amount. So expectation values never mix the even and odd parity sectors.

**Postulate 3 ((R): regularity at the fixed locus).** This is an assumption, not a theorem. It asks that each boundary mode, taken on its own, looks thermal with respect to the black hole's time flow. The temperature is fixed by the gravitational field: it is exactly the one for which the imaginary-time version of the field describes a smooth geometry, with no cone-shaped point.

**Postulate 4 ((HD), the Hadamard deposit premise: the descent's deposited state is a Hadamard state on a Cauchy collar of the descent, for the Dirac field and for the scalar fields of the descended phase; the occupation profile over an admissible reference vacuum stays free data).** Quantum fields in a gravitational field make sense only in well-behaved states, called Hadamard states, which look like empty space at very short distances. The series' account of the universe's earliest moment, the descent, leaves its fields in some state, but nothing in the series yet shows that state is well behaved. This premise assumes it: just after the descent the state is of the well-behaved kind. Since good behaviour, once present, is kept by the equations of motion, every later situation, including black holes formed by collapse, then has well-behaved states, which is what the series' horizon results and the energy of quantum fields need. The premise does not say which well-behaved state it is, how many particles of each energy were left behind, or what pattern of primordial ripples they carry; those remain open inputs.

**Postulate 5 ((N$_{+}$): on the continuum of a horizon's null generators the state of the modes is a gauge-invariant quasi-free ground state for the affine null translations along the generators).** Black holes, the horizon of an expanding universe and the horizon an accelerating observer sees all have a temperature. The series used to assume each temperature with its own premise. This premise replaces them with one assumption that mentions no temperature: the quantum modes running along each horizon's light rays are in their lowest-energy state for sliding along the rays. The store proves that this state, seen from one side of the horizon, is exactly thermal at the expected temperature. The assumption describes a black hole that has always existed in equilibrium; read on the light rays falling into the horizon, its short-distance form also covers a hole formed by collapse, given a simplification of the radiating fields to one direction per light ray. For the horizon an accelerating observer sees, and for the cosmological horizon with fields that keep their form under rescalings of lengths, it amounts to choosing the standard vacuum of the field theory. It still carries an input: that the state is smooth where the two halves of the horizon meet, the condition every derivation of black-hole temperature uses in some form. A black hole that has always existed in equilibrium is an idealisation in a universe with a positive cosmological constant, where no such equilibrium state exists, so the local form of the assumption is the one that matters there; whether the series' states are smooth enough at short distances to give that local form everywhere is still open, though it now comes down to the usual short-distance requirement on states: how such states look at very short distances where gravity is present, assumed before, is now proved.

## Temperature and Hawking radiation

This section goes from the shape of the horizon to the light it emits, and then to the state of the modes that emit it.

Everything starts with a single number describing how strongly the horizon pulls. Surface gravity is the redshift-corrected acceleration needed to hover just outside the horizon; with $f = 1 - 2M/r$ the norm of the static Killing field, \[ \kappa = \lim_{r \to 2M} \frac{f'(r)}{2} = \frac{1}{4M} \] in natural units.

This rate will set the temperature.

**Theorem 6 ($\kappa = 1/4M$, computed).** Surface gravity measures how strongly a black hole's horizon pulls, as judged from far away. It is found by taking the acceleration an observer needs to hover at fixed distance, correcting it for the gravitational redshift, and following that quantity down to the horizon. For a non-rotating black hole of mass M the result is 1/(4M) in natural units.

A rate becomes a temperature through a period, and the field supplies one. Replacing time by an imaginary variable $\tau$ closes the time direction into a circle, and near the horizon the section looks like a plane in polar coordinates with angle $\kappa\tau$.

It is smooth there only if the circle has length $2\pi/\kappa$; any other length leaves a cone point, so the period is fixed rather than chosen.

**Theorem 7 (The Euclidean section, and its period).** Replacing time by an imaginary variable turns the gravitational field outside a black hole into a field on a flat four-dimensional space without time, in which the time direction closes into a circle. Near the horizon the geometry this field describes looks like a flat plane in polar coordinates, but only if the circle has exactly the right length, $8\pi$ times the mass in natural units; any other length leaves a cone point. So the period is fixed by the gravitational field rather than chosen.

The next step connects this geometry to the algebra of the boundary modes. The curvature of the gravitational gauge field picks out, with no arbitrary choice, the plane of the boost bivector $\sigma_r$, and at the horizon the rate of that boost is the surface gravity $\kappa = 1/4M$.

That rate converts the algebra's natural angle into ordinary time: an interval of $2\pi$ in rapidity is $2\pi/\kappa = 8\pi M$ of Schwarzschild time. This single conversion factor is where the numerical value of the temperature enters.

**Theorem 8 (The plane and the rate).** The curvature of a non-rotating black hole picks out, at every distance from the centre, a particular pair of planes without any arbitrary choice. At the horizon one of these is the plane in which the horizon's own time symmetry acts as a boost, and the rate of that boost is the surface gravity, one over four times the mass in natural units. That rate is the single number converting the geometry's natural angle into ordinary time, and it is where the numerical value of the black hole's temperature comes from.

One might hope the algebra alone could fix the temperature, and this result shows it cannot. A symmetry $\theta$ of a single mode's algebra that respects the flow fixes every equilibrium state, and one that reverses the flow only flips the sign: \[ \omega_\beta \circ \theta = \omega_{-\beta} \]

So the size $|\beta|$ of the inverse temperature depends on how the flow's parameter is converted into physical time, which is exactly what the geometric rate supplies.

**Theorem 9 (Rate rigidity: the temperature is not algebra intrinsic).** For a single quantum mode with a given time evolution, the symmetries of the algebra that respect the time evolution, or reverse it, can at most flip the sign of an equilibrium state's inverse temperature; they never change its size. Rescaling the clock rescales the temperature while leaving the state itself alone. So the algebra on its own cannot pick out a particular temperature: that needs outside information, namely how the evolution's parameter is converted into physical time.

**Theorem 10 (The rate, and the discharge of hypothesis (iii)).** Two physical premises, one restricting which states are allowed and one requiring smoothness of the geometry at the horizon, force each boundary mode of the black hole into a thermal state. Every mode comes out at the same temperature, the Hawking temperature, without any assumption about correlations between the modes. As a by-product, a condition that previously had to be assumed, that each mode is genuinely entangled with its partner, now follows from the premises.

## Where the state comes from

This section turns from the horizon to the state itself: where the series' states come from, and whether they are of the well-behaved kind the horizon results need.

Everything so far assumes that the series' states are Hadamard in the first place. The premise (HD) adopts this: the state the descent deposits is a Hadamard state on a Cauchy collar of the descent, for the Dirac and scalar fields, with its occupation profile left free.

Because the Hadamard form propagates, every later field then carries Hadamard states, and with (HS) the local form of (N$_{+}$) follows at every horizon. The premise gives neither which state, nor the ground-state clause, nor the spectrum of primordial ripples.

The first test is the kind of deposit the series uses, a thermal one. ## What the Hadamard condition leaves free

The last section asks what the Hadamard condition leaves undecided about the deposit.

One might hope that the Hadamard condition also fixes the pattern of primordial ripples. It does not: relative to any homogeneous Hadamard reference, a homogeneous deposit can have any spectrum on any bounded band of scales.

What it forbids is a red law on a band reaching $k = 0$ for slice fields of nonzero mean, and, for the conformally coupled field, a power law on a band unbounded toward short scales. So a red band needs two ends, and the condition does not say where they are.

**Theorem 28 (Measured against a homogeneous Hadamard reference state, the Hadamard condition leaves a homogeneous deposit's spectrum free on every bounded band of scales; for the conformally coupled field it allows a power-law ratio on no band unbounded toward short scales, and, for slice fields of nonzero mean, a red law on no band reaching k = 0, so a red band needs an ultraviolet and an infrared end, neither of which the condition fixes).** The series needs the quantum state left by the descent to be of a regular kind, called Hadamard, so that energies and horizon physics make sense. One might hope that this regularity would also fix the pattern of primordial ripples seen in the sky. This result shows it does not. The Hadamard condition only controls the shortest distances; over any finite range of scales, including the whole range astronomers measure, the state can have any strength and any tilt of ripples. What the condition and the existence of the state do require is that a pattern tilted like the observed one must stop somewhere at very short scales and somewhere at very long ones, and nothing in the condition says where.
